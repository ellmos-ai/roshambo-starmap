# Reading the map

This chart shows a night sky that does not exist. Every constellation on it was
invented by the agents who built this project, and nothing here claims to describe
the real sky. The map borrows the vocabulary of astronomy — right ascension,
declination, magnitude — only as a convenient way to place points on a sphere.
Do not look for these stars overhead; you will not find them.

**The bands.** Each constellation was given its own strip of sky to live in, so
the figures do not pile up on one another. The northern constellations sit in
right-ascension bands of forty degrees each; the southern ones share wider bands
closer to the pole. That is why the map reads as neighbourhoods rather than a
crowd.

**The projection.** The sky is a sphere and the page is flat, so the map uses a
stereographic projection centred on the north celestial pole. Declination circles
become rings around the middle of the canvas; the far south gathers toward the
edges.

**The dots.** Each star is drawn as a small disc. Two things are encoded in it:

- *Size* is brightness, on the magnitude scale: the bigger the dot, the brighter
  the star. The scale runs backwards compared to everyday intuition — low and
  negative magnitudes are the bright ones. The legend's three example dots show
  what bright, middling, and faint look like.
- *Colour* follows the star's invented spectral class, from blue-white for the
  hot young ones to orange-red for the cool old ones.

**The lines.** Thin segments connect a constellation's stars into its figure —
a crane, an anchor, a cairn. They are there to help the eye group the dots, the
same way stick figures do on real star charts.

**The faint specks.** Scattered between the named figures are many small, dim
stars with no names and no lines, and a soft river of even fainter light curving
across the sky. They are depth and texture, not destinations.

**The frame.** Cardinal letters and tick marks sit against the edges for
orientation only; a title block and legend in the corner list every constellation
present and restate, plainly, that this sky is made up.
